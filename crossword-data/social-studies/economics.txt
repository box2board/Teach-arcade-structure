[economics--basic-economic-concepts]
Scarcity|Limited resources relative to people's wants.
Economics|Study of allocating scarce resources.
Labor|Human effort used in production.
Capital|Tools, equipment, and buildings used to produce goods.
Land|Factor of production including natural resources.
Incentive|Potential reward or penalty influencing a choice.
Service|Activity performed to meet a want or need.
Goods|Tangible products made to satisfy wants or needs.
Specialization|Focusing production on particular tasks or products.
Trade Off|Giving up something to obtain something else.
Opportunity Cost|Value of the best alternative forgone.
Productivity|Output per unit of input, such as per worker-hour.
Entrepreneurship|Organizing resources while taking business risks.
Marginal|Describes benefits or costs of one additional unit or action.
Exchange|Transfer of goods or services between participants.
PPF|Initials for the frontier showing two-output production trade-offs.
Technology|Methods and knowledge that can improve productive capacity.
Efficiency|Using resources without avoidable waste.
Positive|Describes an economic claim that can be tested using evidence.
Allocation|Distribution of available resources among alternative uses.
[economics--opportunity-cost]
Opportunity Cost|Value of the best alternative given up.
Trade Off|Sacrifice made to obtain something else.
Time|Resource used even when an activity requires no money.
Explicit Cost|Direct monetary payment for an activity.
Implicit Cost|Value of a resource's forgone alternative use.
Marginal Cost|Additional cost of one extra unit or action.
Sunk Cost|Unrecoverable expense already incurred.
Wages|Earnings forgone when a student chooses leisure instead of paid work.
Alternative|Another available choice rejected when a decision is made.
Scarcity|Limited resources making choices necessary.
Marginal Benefit|Additional gain from one more unit or action.
Leisure|Nonwork time whose opportunity cost can rise when wages increase.
Production|Making goods or services using resources.
PPF|Initials of the graph showing possible output combinations and trade-offs.
Nonrefundable|Describes a past payment that cannot be returned.
Preferences|Individual priorities helping explain different valuations of alternatives.
Resources|Inputs that can be assigned to competing uses.
Housing|Next-best land use forgone when a town chooses a park in the stated example.
Future|Time period whose benefits and costs should guide a current decision.
Mutually Exclusive|Describes alternatives that cannot all be chosen together.

Command|Describes an economy whose major decisions are directed centrally.
Market|Describes an economy relying heavily on decentralized exchange.
Mixed|Describes an economy combining private exchange and government involvement.
Profit|Business revenue remaining after relevant costs.
Property Rights|Rules concerning ownership, use, and transfer of assets.

Competition|Rivalry that can encourage efficiency and innovation.

Consumers|People purchasing goods and services for use.

Public Ownership|Holding assets through government rather than private organizations.
Private Ownership|Holding assets through individuals or nongovernment organizations.
Regulation|Government rules shaping business activity.
Innovation|Development of new products or production methods.
Distribution|Pattern of who receives an economy's goods or income.
Efficiency|Producing or allocating resources with minimal avoidable waste.
Customs|Inherited practices influencing tasks in a traditional economy.
Output|Quantity of goods and services produced.
Allocation|Assignment of resources to competing purposes.
[economics--supply-and-demand]
Demand|Quantities buyers are willing and able to purchase at different prices.
Supply|Quantities sellers are willing and able to offer at different prices.
Equilibrium|Condition where quantity supplied equals quantity demanded.
Shortage|Quantity demanded exceeding quantity supplied at a given price.
Surplus|Quantity supplied exceeding quantity demanded at a given price.
Price|Amount charged for a product that moves buyers along a demand curve.
Substitute|Good that can replace another in consumption.
Complement|Good used together with another.
Income|Household earnings whose rise usually increases demand for normal goods.
Technology|Improved production methods that can increase supply.
Normal Good|Product whose demand generally rises as consumer income rises.
Quantity Demanded|Amount buyers purchase at one particular price.
Quantity Supplied|Amount sellers offer at one particular price.
Production Costs|Expenses whose increase usually reduces supply at a given price.
Shift|Movement of an entire curve after a factor other than own price changes.
Law of Demand|Principle that higher prices generally reduce purchases, other things equal.
Law of Supply|Principle that higher prices generally increase offerings, other things equal.
Drought|Weather event that can reduce apple harvests and shift their supply left.
Market|System through which buyers and sellers exchange a product.
Ceteris Paribus|Latin phrase meaning other things equal.
[economics--gdp]
GDP|Initials of the measure of domestic final production's market value.
Nominal|Describes output value measured using current prices.
Real|Describes output value adjusted for price changes.
Consumption|GDP spending category including household purchases.
Investment|GDP category including newly produced business equipment.
Exports|Goods and services sold to buyers abroad.
Imports|Goods and services purchased from abroad.
Net Exports|Value calculated by subtracting foreign purchases from foreign sales.
Final Goods|Products counted at their end-use value to avoid double counting.
Per Capita|Phrase meaning per person, used when dividing GDP by population.
Intermediate|Describes inputs such as flour purchased to produce bread.
Double Counting|Error of adding input values again after counting a final product.
Transfer Payments|Government benefits excluded from purchases of current production.
Domestic|Describes production within a country's boundaries.
Inflation|Rising prices that can increase nominal GDP without more output.
Population|Number of people used to calculate average output per person.
Unpaid Work|Useful household labor often omitted from market production measures.
Financial Asset|Ownership claim whose transfer is not itself new production.
Output|Goods and services actually produced during a period.
Well Being|Broad quality of life that GDP does not fully measure.
[economics--inflation]
Inflation|Sustained increase in the general price level.
Deflation|Sustained decrease in the general price level.
Disinflation|Decline in the inflation rate while prices may still rise.
Purchasing Power|Quantity of goods and services a sum of money can buy.
CPI|Initials of the consumer price measure based on a representative basket.
Nominal Wage|Pay measured in current money units.
Real Wage|Pay adjusted for purchasing power.
Price Level|Broad measure of prices across an economy.
Basket|Representative combination of consumer purchases used in a price index.
Fixed Income|Income that may fail to keep up with unexpected price increases.
Expectations|Beliefs about future prices that influence wage and price setting.
Demand Pull|Describes inflation driven by spending growing faster than available output.
Cost Push|Describes inflation arising from broad increases in production expenses.
Central Bank|Institution monitoring inflation and managing monetary policy.
Real Interest Rate|Approximate borrowing rate after subtracting inflation from the nominal rate.
Borrower|Person whose fixed nominal debt can become less burdensome with unexpected inflation.
Debt|Obligation whose real burden can increase during deflation.
Energy|Input whose higher cost can raise expenses across many industries.
Index|Statistical measure comparing price changes with a reference period.
Percentage|Form in which an inflation rate is commonly expressed.
[economics--unemployment]
Unemployed|Classification of people without jobs who are available and actively searching under standard rules.
Labor Force|People classified as employed plus unemployed.
Frictional|Describes unemployment during job transitions and searches.
Structural|Describes unemployment caused by mismatched skills and available jobs.
Cyclical|Describes unemployment associated with economic downturns.
Seasonal|Describes joblessness from predictable annual changes in work.
Recession|Broad economic downturn that can reduce staffing.
Training|Learning new skills that may address a job mismatch.
Employed|Classification that includes people working part-time.
Job Search|Active effort generally required for official unemployment classification.
Discouraged Workers|People who want work but stop searching because jobs seem unavailable.
Participation Rate|Labor force as a share of the relevant population.
Unemployment Rate|Unemployed people as a percentage of the labor force.
Underemployment|Situation in which work provides fewer hours or uses fewer skills than desired.
Layoffs|Job losses resulting from employers reducing staff.
Demand|Purchasing that can fall during downturns and lead to job losses.
Skills|Abilities that may no longer match new job requirements.
Part Time|Describes work with fewer hours than a typical full-time schedule.
Benefit Claims|Requests for assistance that do not equal the official unemployment count.
Outside|Position relative to the labor force of many people who stop actively searching.

Property Tax|Assessment commonly based on real estate's value.

Credit|Amount reducing tax owed rather than taxable income.

Regressive|Describes a tax taking a larger income share from lower-income taxpayers.
Evasion|Illegal avoidance of tax obligations.
Avoidance|Lawful use of provisions to reduce tax obligations.
Revenue|Income governments collect to fund public responsibilities.
Bracket|Income range subject to a specified marginal rate.
Liability|Final amount legally owed in taxes.
Assessed Value|Official property valuation used for many local taxes.
Public Services|Government activities such as schools and roads financed through revenue.
