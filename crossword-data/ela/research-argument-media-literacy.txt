[ela--research-argument-media-literacy--analyzing-arguments-and-evidence]
Argument|Position developed through reasons and evidence.
Claim|Statement of the position a writer wants readers to accept.
Reason|Explanation of why a position deserves support.
Evidence|Facts, observations, or data supporting a position.
Counterclaim|Different position an argument addresses.
Rebuttal|Response explaining limitations in an opposing position.
Relevant|Describes evidence directly connected to the issue being argued.
Sufficient|Describes evidence adequate in amount and quality to support a position.
Sample|Subset of people or cases used to gather information.
Correlation|Association between variables without proof of a cause.
Causation|Relationship in which one factor produces or contributes to an effect.
Representative|Describes a sample reflecting the larger population it concerns.
Selective|Describes use of favorable evidence while omitting known contrary findings.
Scope|Breadth of a claim that must fit the available evidence.
Data|Recorded observations or measurements used as support.
Source|Origin of information that readers can evaluate.
Generalization|Broad conclusion drawn from individual cases.
Irrelevant|Describes a fact that may be accurate but does not address the claim.
Reasoning|Explanation connecting evidence to the position it supports.
Qualification|Limitation added to keep a claim from overstating the evidence.
[ela--research-argument-media-literacy--rhetorical-appeals-and-persuasive-techniques]
Ethos|Appeal based on a speaker's credibility or character.
Pathos|Appeal aimed at an audience's emotions.
Logos|Appeal based on reasoning and evidence.
Credibility|Quality of being believable or trustworthy on a subject.
Emotion|Feeling a vivid personal story may arouse.
Evidence|Information readers should check even when an appeal feels persuasive.
Audience|People a persuasive message is intended to influence.
Bandwagon|Appeal urging a choice because many others supposedly make it.
Repetition|Reuse of a phrase to emphasize a persuasive message.
Hyperbole|Exaggeration such as describing a small fee as a mountain of debt.
Ad Hominem|Fallacy attacking a person instead of addressing their argument.
False Dilemma|Reasoning flaw presenting only two choices when more exist.
Rhetorical Question|Question asked to make a point rather than obtain an answer.
Endorsement|Expression of approval by someone associated with a product or proposal.
Expertise|Relevant specialized knowledge that can strengthen an appeal to credibility.
Empathy|Understanding or sharing another person's feelings.
Vivid Description|Detailed language helping an audience imagine an experience.
Reasoning|Logical explanation connecting support to a proposal.
Persuasion|Attempt to influence another person's beliefs or actions.
Fallacy|Error in reasoning that weakens an argument.
[ela--research-argument-media-literacy--research-and-source-evaluation]
Credibility|Trustworthiness of information for the question being studied.
Expertise|Author's relevant qualifications or specialized knowledge.
Publication Date|Information showing when a source was issued.
Primary Source|Firsthand account or record created by someone directly involved.
Secondary Source|Work analyzing or interpreting information from other evidence.
Citation|Reference identifying where borrowed information came from.
Verification|Checking a claim against traceable evidence.
Bias|Preference or perspective that may shape selection of evidence.
Purpose|Reason a source was created, such as to inform or sell.
Research Question|Focused inquiry guiding the search for information.
Corroboration|Confirmation through independent supporting evidence.
Original Data|Measurements traced to their initial collection rather than repeated claims.
Method|Procedure explaining how a study collected or analyzed information.
Transparency|Openness about evidence, methods, and source details.
Relevance|Connection between a source's content and the specific inquiry.
Conflict of Interest|Personal or financial stake that may affect a source's judgment.
Perspective|Viewpoint of a source's creator, including a firsthand witness.
Context|Background needed to understand a statistic's meaning.
Accuracy|Correctness of information, separate from how useful it is for a narrow question.
References|List of works enabling readers to trace a source's supporting information.
[ela--research-argument-media-literacy--quoting-paraphrasing-and-attribution]
Quotation|Exact wording borrowed from a source and marked as such.
Paraphrase|Restatement preserving an idea in new wording and sentence structure.
Summary|Short account of a source's main points.
Attribution|Identification of whose words or ideas are being used.
Citation|Reference identifying the source of borrowed material.
Quotation Marks|Punctuation enclosing directly borrowed words.
Plagiarism|Presenting someone else's words or ideas as one's own.
Source|Original work from which information is borrowed.
Analysis|Writer's explanation connecting borrowed evidence to a claim.
Context|Surrounding information needed to preserve a quoted passage's meaning.
Signal Phrase|Introductory wording identifying a speaker or writer before borrowed material.
Bibliography|List of sources usually appearing at the end of a work.
Exact Wording|Language preserved without changes when it matters to the discussion.
Misrepresentation|Distortion caused by removing a phrase that changes a source's meaning.
Integration|Fitting a quotation smoothly into the writer's own sentence or paragraph.
Patchwriting|Restating a source by changing only a few words while keeping its structure.
Overstatement|Making a source's cautious claim sound more certain than it is.
Note Taking|Recording information while separating copied wording from personal restatements.
Interpretation|Writer's own explanation that should be distinguished from a source's contribution.
Relevance|Connection between selected borrowed information and the point being supported.
