Moby Dick (1956) Teacher Guide

Plan the lesson and review the answer keys for both student formats.

Teaching Notes

Learning goals & preparation

Essential question: When does a leader’s private goal become a danger to everyone else?

  • Explain how obsession & leadership shapes character choices.
  • Support an interpretation with two accurately described scenes.
  • Compare the portrayal with a selected passage from its source or another relevant text.

Use the 1956 film. Introduce narrator, symbol, obsession, foreshadowing, and whaling. Students do not need to reproduce hunting details. Prepare a teacher-selected novel excerpt and discuss stereotyped portrayals of non-European people critically.

Content considerations & viewing plan
  • Whale hunting, harpoons, animal injury, and intense sea peril.
  • Drowning, deaths, threatening leadership, and frightening final images.
  • Dated racial or cultural stereotypes and religious imagery.

Preview the assigned edition and arrange required permissions and an alternative source-based assignment. Students can explain harm without describing graphic acts or repeating slurs. Keep discussion focused on characters; do not require personal disclosures.

Pause after the departure, after Ahab announces his goal, during Starbuck’s objections, and following the final pursuit.

Use brief responses during viewing and reserve interpretation for afterward. Check subtitles and captions. If scenes are omitted, bridge the narrative and adapt the affected questions.

Context & source comparison

The film greatly condenses a long novel that includes many reflective and technical chapters. Its whale is a dramatic figure, not a reliable lesson on animal motives or marine biology. Compare Ishmael’s narrative voice or a confrontation between Ahab and Starbuck with the novel. Discuss historical whaling using sources beyond the film.

For a short comparison, select one accessible source or excerpt, establish author/date/purpose, and compare one film claim with what it supports and leaves unanswered. Teacher selection and adaptation are required.

AFI credits and synopsis

Discussion & optional extensions
  • When does a leader’s private goal become a danger to everyone else?
  • Which scene best supports your interpretation, and what might another viewer notice?
  • What does the film leave out or simplify?

Exit ticket: One claim, one supporting scene, and one question for further study.

Optional extension: Build an evidence chart with columns for film portrayal, external source, agreement or difference, and remaining uncertainty. Assess accuracy and reasoning.

Paper Worksheet Answer Key

Expected answers for 13 questions. Accept supported alternatives for analytical prompts.

  1. What is the difference between commitment and obsession?
    Commitment can respond to evidence and responsibilities; obsession may override them.
  2. Why does Ishmael seek a voyage?
    He wants to leave his unsettled life ashore and go to sea.
  3. How does Ishmael’s friendship with Queequeg challenge an initial assumption?
    Shared experience builds trust despite his first reaction to unfamiliar appearance and customs.
  4. What does Father Mapple’s sermon add to the opening?
    It introduces religious language about duty and consequence that foreshadows later conflict.
  5. What goal does Ahab announce to the crew?
    He wants to find and kill the white whale that injured him.
  6. How does the promised reward encourage participation?
    The gold coin turns his private pursuit into a visible incentive for the crew.
  7. Why does Starbuck question Ahab’s purpose?
    He sees revenge as a misuse of the voyage and a threat to their responsibilities.
  8. What happens when ordinary work competes with the pursuit?
    Ahab increasingly prioritizes the whale over commercial duties and safety.
  9. How does Queequeg’s coffin change its function?
    An object associated with expected death later becomes a means of survival.
  10. Why can Ishmael tell the story afterward?
    He survives, supported by the floating coffin, while the others are lost.
  11. Choose a moment when Starbuck represents an alternative kind of leadership.
    Accept an objection grounded in duty, safety, or responsibility with scene evidence.
  12. What is lost when a long novel becomes a shorter adventure film?
    Narrative reflection, technical passages, and multiple perspectives may be reduced.
  13. Why should the whale’s behavior not be treated as a scientific explanation?
    The film uses dramatic personification and symbolism; factual claims need reliable marine-science sources.
Online Worksheet Answer Key

Expected answers for 20 questions. Accept supported alternatives for analytical prompts.

  1. What is the difference between commitment and obsession?
    Commitment can respond to evidence and responsibilities; obsession may override them.
  2. How can a narrator shape an audience’s view of a leader?
    Selection, description, and perspective influence what motives and actions seem important.
  3. Why does Ishmael seek a voyage?
    He wants to leave his unsettled life ashore and go to sea.
  4. How does Ishmael’s friendship with Queequeg challenge an initial assumption?
    Shared experience builds trust despite his first reaction to unfamiliar appearance and customs.
  5. What does Father Mapple’s sermon add to the opening?
    It introduces religious language about duty and consequence that foreshadows later conflict.
  6. How does Ahab’s delayed appearance affect expectations?
    The absent captain becomes mysterious before his authority is directly visible.
  7. What goal does Ahab announce to the crew?
    He wants to find and kill the white whale that injured him.
  8. How does the promised reward encourage participation?
    The gold coin turns his private pursuit into a visible incentive for the crew.
  9. Why does Starbuck question Ahab’s purpose?
    He sees revenge as a misuse of the voyage and a threat to their responsibilities.
  10. How does Ahab interpret the whale differently from a practical whaler?
    He gives it a larger hostile meaning rather than treating it only as an animal.
  11. What happens when ordinary work competes with the pursuit?
    Ahab increasingly prioritizes the whale over commercial duties and safety.
  12. How do encounters with other ships offer opportunities to reconsider?
    Other crews bring information, loss, or warnings that could change the captain’s judgment.
  13. How does Queequeg’s coffin change its function?
    An object associated with expected death later becomes a means of survival.
  14. What do warnings about the whale reveal about Ahab’s leadership?
    He hears danger but continues to make the pursuit his overriding purpose.
  15. How does the final hunt affect the crew?
    The pursuit leads to destruction and loss rather than the security Ahab promises.
  16. Why can Ishmael tell the story afterward?
    He survives, supported by the floating coffin, while the others are lost.
  17. Choose a moment when Starbuck represents an alternative kind of leadership.
    Accept an objection grounded in duty, safety, or responsibility with scene evidence.
  18. How might the whale support more than one symbolic reading?
    It can represent nature, mystery, or a meaning Ahab projects onto it; require evidence rather than one fixed answer.
  19. What is lost when a long novel becomes a shorter adventure film?
    Narrative reflection, technical passages, and multiple perspectives may be reduced.
  20. Why should the whale’s behavior not be treated as a scientific explanation?
    The film uses dramatic personification and symbolism; factual claims need reliable marine-science sources.

Final writing: Look for a clear claim, two accurately described scenes, reasoning, and a meaningful limitation of the portrayal or a question for further study. Accept different supported interpretations. No graphic descriptions are required.
