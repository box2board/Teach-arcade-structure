Teaching Notes

Learning goals & preparation

Essential question: How does Jane defend her dignity when belonging comes at a cost?

  • Explain how agency & dignity shapes character choices.
  • Support an interpretation with two accurately described scenes.
  • Compare the portrayal with a selected passage from its source or another relevant text.

Use the 2011 adaptation. Introduce Gothic convention, flashback, narrative voice, and agency. Provide a selected Brontë passage; discuss the film’s portrayal of Bertha with attention to missing perspectives.

Content considerations & viewing plan
  • Child abuse, harsh punishment, illness, death, and intense isolation.
  • An interrupted wedding reveals a concealed marriage; sexual themes and brief nude imagery in artwork.
  • Fire, injury, a fatal fall, and a dehumanizing portrayal of confinement and mental distress.

Preview the assigned edition and arrange required permissions and an alternative source-based assignment. Students can explain harm without describing graphic acts or repeating slurs. Keep discussion focused on characters; do not require personal disclosures.

Pause after the Lowood sequence, after the proposal, after the wedding interruption, and before Jane’s return.

Use brief responses during viewing and reserve interpretation for afterward. Check subtitles and captions. If scenes are omitted, bridge the narrative and adapt the affected questions.

Context & source comparison

The film opens with Jane’s flight and uses flashbacks rather than following the novel’s chronology throughout. Consider whose interior life is developed and whose is withheld. Avoid using literary depictions as diagnoses.

For a short comparison, select one accessible source or excerpt, establish author/date/purpose, and compare one film claim with what it supports and leaves unanswered. Teacher selection and adaptation are required.

Discussion & optional extensions
  • How does Jane defend her dignity when belonging comes at a cost?
  • Which scene best supports your interpretation, and what might another viewer notice?
  • What does the film leave out or simplify?

Exit ticket: One claim, one supporting scene, and one question for further study.

Optional extension: Build an evidence chart with columns for film portrayal, external source, agreement or difference, and remaining uncertainty. Assess accuracy and reasoning.

Paper Worksheet Answer Key

Expected answers for 13 questions. Accept supported alternatives for analytical prompts.

  1. What can a flashback change about a viewer’s experience?
    It delays context and invites the viewer to reinterpret events as earlier information emerges.
  2. What does the opening flight make us wonder about Jane?
    Her distress raises questions about what she has escaped and what choices brought her there.
  3. How does Jane’s childhood establish a struggle for dignity?
    Exclusion and punishment show how others deny her fairness and a secure place.
  4. How do the Lowood scenes portray institutional power?
    Rules and harsh treatment reveal how authority can discipline vulnerable children.
  5. How do the moors contribute to the film’s atmosphere?
    Their scale and exposure emphasize isolation, uncertainty, and the difficulty of Jane’s journey.
  6. How does Jane’s position as a governess complicate belonging?
    She works within a wealthy household without having the status or security of its owners.
  7. What makes Jane and Rochester’s conversations significant?
    She speaks with independence, challenging expectations based on gender and class.
  8. Why does Jane question whether Rochester treats her as an equal?
    His status and the social expectations around him make apparent affection difficult to interpret.
  9. What interrupts the wedding?
    The revelation that Rochester is already married prevents the planned marriage.
  10. How does Jane’s changed financial position affect her return?
    Greater independence gives her more freedom to decide rather than depend on Rochester for security.
  11. Which choice best demonstrates Jane’s agency?
    Support an example in which she acts according to her values despite pressure or loss.
  12. How does a selected novel passage differ in conveying Jane’s interior voice?
    Compare first-person language with performance, silence, framing, or dialogue in the film.
  13. Whose perspective could complicate the ending or Thornfield’s story?
    Bertha’s withheld viewpoint is one example; explain the limitation rather than inventing her thoughts as fact.
Online Worksheet Answer Key

Expected answers for 20 questions. Accept supported alternatives for analytical prompts.

  1. What can a flashback change about a viewer’s experience?
    It delays context and invites the viewer to reinterpret events as earlier information emerges.
  2. How might a Gothic setting create uncertainty?
    Isolation, darkness, strange sounds, and concealed spaces can suggest threats or secrets.
  3. What does the opening flight make us wonder about Jane?
    Her distress raises questions about what she has escaped and what choices brought her there.
  4. How does Jane’s childhood establish a struggle for dignity?
    Exclusion and punishment show how others deny her fairness and a secure place.
  5. How do the Lowood scenes portray institutional power?
    Rules and harsh treatment reveal how authority can discipline vulnerable children.
  6. What does Helen’s friendship offer Jane?
    It provides companionship and a different way to respond to hardship.
  7. How do the moors contribute to the film’s atmosphere?
    Their scale and exposure emphasize isolation, uncertainty, and the difficulty of Jane’s journey.
  8. How does Jane’s position as a governess complicate belonging?
    She works within a wealthy household without having the status or security of its owners.
  9. What makes Jane and Rochester’s conversations significant?
    She speaks with independence, challenging expectations based on gender and class.
  10. How do strange events at Thornfield build suspense?
    Unexplained sounds and incidents suggest information withheld from Jane and the viewer.
  11. Why does Jane question whether Rochester treats her as an equal?
    His status and the social expectations around him make apparent affection difficult to interpret.
  12. How does the proposal appear to change Jane’s future?
    It promises belonging and recognition while leaving a crucial secret concealed.
  13. What interrupts the wedding?
    The revelation that Rochester is already married prevents the planned marriage.
  14. Why does Jane leave despite loving Rochester?
    She refuses a relationship that violates her principles and compromises her independence.
  15. Why does St. John’s proposal fail to meet Jane’s needs?
    A duty-based marriage does not provide the relationship or self-direction she seeks.
  16. How does Jane’s changed financial position affect her return?
    Greater independence gives her more freedom to decide rather than depend on Rochester for security.
  17. Which choice best demonstrates Jane’s agency?
    Support an example in which she acts according to her values despite pressure or loss.
  18. How does the film’s reordered narrative affect sympathy or suspense?
    Explain a specific effect of seeing Jane’s flight before understanding its cause.
  19. How does a selected novel passage differ in conveying Jane’s interior voice?
    Compare first-person language with performance, silence, framing, or dialogue in the film.
  20. Whose perspective could complicate the ending or Thornfield’s story?
    Bertha’s withheld viewpoint is one example; explain the limitation rather than inventing her thoughts as fact.

Final writing: Look for a clear claim, two accurately described scenes, reasoning, and a meaningful limitation of the portrayal or a question for further study. Accept different supported interpretations. No graphic descriptions are required.
