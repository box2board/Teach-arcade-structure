Teaching Notes

Learning goals & preparation

Essential question: What does it take for freedom to be recognized by law when people in power deny it?

  • Explain how evidence of kidnapping and origin challenges claims of ownership.
  • Analyze the roles of resistance, translation, advocacy, and legal argument.
  • Distinguish the film’s dramatic interpretation from the historical record and the limited scope of the ruling.

Before viewing, introduce slavery versus the Atlantic slave trade, abolition, the Middle Passage, testimony, salvage claims, treaties, appeals, and judicial independence. Explain that legal claims treating people as property are being studied critically, not accepted as valid judgments of human worth.

Sengbe Pieh is called Cinque in the film. Many of the Africans were Mende-speaking. Avoid treating West Africa as a single culture or country.

Content considerations & viewing plan

Preview the violent opening and the later account of the Middle Passage, including nudity and implied sexual violence. Arrange required permissions and an alternative using historical documents before assigning the film.

Tell students they can explain kidnapping and resistance without describing graphic acts. Avoid reenactments of enslavement, slave auctions, or debates asking students to defend a person’s right to own another human. Do not ask students to disclose personal trauma.

Suggested narrative pauses: after the revolt and competing claims; after the defense establishes communication; after the evidence and testimony; and after the appeal and Supreme Court decision. Verify stopping points in your own edition; these are not equal-length periods or exact timestamps.

Use short responses during viewing and reserve analysis for afterward. Some scenes use Mende with subtitles, while others deliberately leave dialogue untranslated; check caption readability. If scenes are omitted, provide a brief factual bridge and adapt affected questions.

Historical context & source comparison

Separate the transatlantic voyage aboard the Tecora from the later Cuban coastal voyage aboard La Amistad. The 1841 ruling recognized these Africans’ freedom; it did not abolish slavery nationwide.

Theodore Joadson is a fictional/composite character. Do not treat his dialogue or actions as documentary evidence. Nor should Adams’s filmed speech substitute for the actual legal record.

Optional source comparison: Use the National Archives’ Amistad case materials. Select an accessible document or short transcription, establish author/date/purpose, then compare one film claim with what the document supports and what it leaves unanswered. Teacher selection and adaptation of the source are required.

Research links: National Archives case document · National Archives historical analysis · Office of the Historian overview · Paramount’s film/composite-character notes.

Discussion & optional extensions
  • How does translation change whose account the court can hear?
  • Where does the film show the Africans directing their own struggle rather than relying only on allies?
  • How can an individual legal victory coexist with a wider system of injustice?

Exit ticket: One claim about freedom, one supporting scene, and one question for the historical record.

Optional extension: Create a case timeline or an evidence chart distinguishing testimony, documents, political pressure, and legal conclusions. Assess accuracy and reasoning rather than artistic polish.

Paper Worksheet Answer Key

Expected answers for the 13-question PDF. Accept supported alternatives for analytical questions.

  1. How is ending the slave trade different from ending slavery?
    Ending the trade stops the trafficking of people across the Atlantic; abolition ends the institution of slavery. One does not automatically accomplish the other.
  2. What does Cinque want after the Africans take control of La Amistad?
    He leads resistance and demands that the surviving sailors take them back to Africa. Their goal is to regain freedom and return home.
  3. Why do the Africans end up in U.S. custody?
    The surviving sailors redirect the voyage; U.S. naval officers take control of the ship and the Africans are detained.
  4. Name one claim made about the Africans or the ship. Who makes it?
    Ruiz and Montes assert ownership; naval officers seek salvage compensation; Spain demands return under treaty arguments. Accept two accurately identified claims without endorsing their treatment of people as property.
  5. Why does Baldwin need to establish where the Africans came from?
    Their origins challenge the claim that they were legally held as Spanish property. Illegal kidnapping and trafficking support their claim to freedom.
  6. How does James Covey help the Africans and their lawyers?
    He enables communication with the lawyers and translation of the Africans’ accounts, allowing their voices to inform the defense.
  7. What does Cinque’s account reveal about his capture? No graphic detail is needed.
    He was kidnapped in Africa and trafficked against his will; he had a home and family before enslavement.
  8. How are the voyages on the Tecora and La Amistad different?
    The Tecora carried kidnapped Africans across the Atlantic to Cuba. La Amistad was the later coastal voyage within Cuba on which the revolt occurred; it was not the transatlantic ship.
  9. Name one political or diplomatic pressure on Van Buren’s administration.
    Spanish demands and concern about domestic political support, especially among supporters of slavery, create pressure against releasing the Africans.
  10. How does Adams connect the case to American ideals of liberty?
    He challenges the contradiction between professed ideals of liberty and efforts to return kidnapped people to captivity. Support with an accurately described argument; a verbatim quotation is unnecessary.
  11. What does the Supreme Court decide? Does this end U.S. slavery?
    It recognizes their freedom, rejecting the claim that the illegally kidnapped Africans must be returned as Spanish property. Distinguish this group’s legal status from slavery throughout the United States. It does not abolish slavery nationwide.
  12. Choose one scene. Explain how it shows a struggle for freedom in 2–3 sentences.
    Accept a specific scene and reasoning: competing ownership claims versus testimony about home and family, for example. Evaluate explanation rather than graphic detail.
  13. What film detail would you verify? Name a historical source that could help.
    Accept a focused question and an appropriate source such as court records, contemporary letters, or archival research. Students might investigate the fictional/composite Theodore Joadson or compare Adams’s film argument with the record.
Online Worksheet Answer Key

Expected answers for the 20-question online worksheet. Accept supported alternatives for analytical questions.

  1. What is the difference between ending the Atlantic slave trade and ending slavery itself?
    Ending the trade stops the trafficking of people across the Atlantic; abolition ends the institution of slavery. One does not automatically accomplish the other.
  2. Why might a court need both documents and testimony to decide what happened to a person?
    Documents can establish a record, while testimony can explain experience or challenge that record. Consider origin, reliability, and whose voice is missing.
  3. What does Cinque do during the revolt, and what destination do the Africans want to reach?
    He leads resistance and demands that the surviving sailors take them back to Africa. Their goal is to regain freedom and return home.
  4. How do the Africans end up in U.S. custody instead of returning home?
    The surviving sailors redirect the voyage; U.S. naval officers take control of the ship and the Africans are detained.
  5. Identify two competing claims made about the Africans or the ship. What does each claimant want?
    Ruiz and Montes assert ownership; naval officers seek salvage compensation; Spain demands return under treaty arguments. Accept two accurately identified claims without endorsing their treatment of people as property.
  6. Why does Roger Baldwin investigate whether the Africans were born free in Africa rather than enslaved in Cuba?
    Their origins challenge the claim that they were legally held as Spanish property. Illegal kidnapping and trafficking support their claim to freedom.
  7. How does the language barrier affect the Africans’ ability to explain their case?
    They cannot initially communicate their account to English-speaking lawyers and the court. Lack of translation limits access to evidence, not their intelligence or humanity.
  8. What does James Covey make possible once he can translate?
    He enables communication with the lawyers and translation of the Africans’ accounts, allowing their voices to inform the defense.
  9. What does Cinque’s account reveal about how he was taken from his home? You do not need to describe violence.
    He was kidnapped in Africa and trafficked against his will; he had a home and family before enslavement.
  10. How is the Atlantic voyage on the Tecora different from the voyage on La Amistad?
    The Tecora carried kidnapped Africans across the Atlantic to Cuba. La Amistad was the later coastal voyage within Cuba on which the revolt occurred; it was not the transatlantic ship.
  11. How do the ship records or other documents help the defense challenge the claimed origins of the Africans?
    Records help trace the trafficking route and support the account that they recently came from Africa, challenging claims that they were legally enslaved Cuban residents. Accept a relevant document accurately described.
  12. What does Cinque’s repeated demand for freedom reveal about his role in the case?
    He presses his own claim and communicates the Africans’ purpose. They are active participants rather than merely people whom others rescue.
  13. What political or diplomatic pressures does the film show influencing President Van Buren’s administration?
    Spanish demands and concern about domestic political support, especially among supporters of slavery, create pressure against releasing the Africans.
  14. After a lower court rules for the Africans, why is their freedom still uncertain?
    The government appeals. A favorable lower-court ruling does not end the legal dispute or immediately resolve their future.
  15. How does John Quincy Adams connect the Africans’ case to American ideals of liberty?
    He challenges the contradiction between professed ideals of liberty and efforts to return kidnapped people to captivity. Support with an accurately described argument; a verbatim quotation is unnecessary.
  16. What does the Supreme Court’s decision mean for the Africans whose freedom is being contested?
    It recognizes their freedom, rejecting the claim that the illegally kidnapped Africans must be returned as Spanish property. Distinguish this group’s legal status from slavery throughout the United States.
  17. Why would it be incorrect to say that this decision ended slavery in the United States?
    The decision resolved these Africans’ case; it did not abolish slavery nationwide. The institution continued after 1841.
  18. Choose a scene that contrasts treating people as property with recognizing their humanity. Explain the contrast.
    Accept a specific scene and reasoning: competing ownership claims versus testimony about home and family, for example. Evaluate explanation rather than graphic detail.
  19. How do the Africans’ actions and the work of translators, lawyers, or abolitionists contribute differently to the struggle for freedom?
    The Africans resist, communicate, testify, and demand freedom; allies provide translation, legal representation, and organized support. A strong response recognizes both agency and collaboration.
  20. Choose a person, event, or argument in the film to verify. What historical source would help, and what would you look for?
    Accept a focused question and an appropriate source such as court records, contemporary letters, or archival research. Students might investigate the fictional/composite Theodore Joadson or compare Adams’s film argument with the record.

Final writing: Look for a clear claim, two accurately described scenes, reasoning connecting those scenes to the struggle for freedom, and one limitation of film as historical evidence. Accept differing supported interpretations. Do not require graphic descriptions.
