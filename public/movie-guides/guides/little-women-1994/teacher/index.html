Teaching Notes

Learning goals & preparation

Essential question: How do the sisters negotiate personal ambitions and the expectations placed on women?

  • Explain how women’s opportunities shapes character choices.
  • Support an interpretation with two accurately described scenes.
  • Compare the portrayal with a selected passage from its source or another relevant text.

Use Gillian Armstrong’s 1994 film, not the 2019 adaptation. Track Meg, Jo, Beth, and Amy with a character chart. Introduce adaptation, independence, vocation, and characterization. A novel passage must be supplied by the teacher.

Content considerations & viewing plan
  • Serious illness and the death of a beloved family member.
  • A frightening fall through ice and family separation during wartime.
  • Romantic kissing and mild language or references.

Preview the assigned edition and arrange required permissions and an alternative source-based assignment. Students can explain harm without describing graphic acts or repeating slurs. Keep discussion focused on characters; do not require personal disclosures.

Pause after early household conflicts, after the skating incident, during the sisters’ adult choices, and following Jo’s final work.

Use brief responses during viewing and reserve interpretation for afterward. Check subtitles and captions. If scenes are omitted, bridge the narrative and adapt the affected questions.

Context & source comparison

The film condenses Alcott’s novel and selects which experiences shape each sister. Compare a passage with its scene to examine the effect of dialogue, performance, and omitted narration. Distinguish the fictional March family from Alcott’s biography rather than assuming every event is literal autobiography.

For a short comparison, select one accessible source or excerpt, establish author/date/purpose, and compare one film claim with what it supports and leaves unanswered. Teacher selection and adaptation are required.

Discussion & optional extensions
  • How do the sisters negotiate personal ambitions and the expectations placed on women?
  • Which scene best supports your interpretation, and what might another viewer notice?
  • What does the film leave out or simplify?

Exit ticket: One claim, one supporting scene, and one question for further study.

Optional extension: Build an evidence chart with columns for film portrayal, external source, agreement or difference, and remaining uncertainty. Assess accuracy and reasoning.

Paper Worksheet Answer Key

Expected answers for 13 questions. Accept supported alternatives for analytical prompts.

  1. How can two siblings value different futures without one being less worthy?
    Different interests and circumstances can lead to different meaningful choices.
  2. How do the sisters make entertainment in their household?
    They create and perform stories and plays together.
  3. What does Jo’s writing reveal about her ambitions?
    She wants creative independence and opportunities beyond expected domestic roles.
  4. How does the family’s response to neighbors’ need reveal its values?
    They share limited resources and prioritize care over comfort.
  5. Why does Amy destroy Jo’s manuscript?
    She acts in anger after being excluded, harming work that matters deeply to Jo.
  6. How does the skating emergency change Jo and Amy’s conflict?
    Danger shifts attention toward their relationship and the need to respond to hurt constructively.
  7. What does Meg’s experience at a fashionable gathering reveal?
    Wealth and social expectations complicate her sense of belonging and self-worth.
  8. Why does Jo reject Laurie’s proposal?
    She does not see their relationship as the marriage he wants, despite deep affection.
  9. How does Amy’s interest in art connect with questions about opportunity?
    Training, travel, and social resources influence the future she can pursue.
  10. What possibilities does the ending create at Plumfield?
    A school and new relationships connect creative work with community life.
  11. Compare the ambitions of two sisters using scenes.
    Accept distinct goals and constraints rather than ranking one life as automatically superior.
  12. What would you examine when comparing a novel passage with this adaptation?
    Whose viewpoint is emphasized, what is omitted, and how visual choices affect meaning.
  13. How does Jo’s authorship help shape the meaning of the family story?
    Writing preserves experience while also selecting and interpreting it.
Online Worksheet Answer Key

Expected answers for 20 questions. Accept supported alternatives for analytical prompts.

  1. How can two siblings value different futures without one being less worthy?
    Different interests and circumstances can lead to different meaningful choices.
  2. What can a film use instead of a novel’s narrator to reveal character?
    Performance, dialogue, framing, music, and selected actions.
  3. How do the sisters make entertainment in their household?
    They create and perform stories and plays together.
  4. What does Jo’s writing reveal about her ambitions?
    She wants creative independence and opportunities beyond expected domestic roles.
  5. How does the family’s response to neighbors’ need reveal its values?
    They share limited resources and prioritize care over comfort.
  6. How does Laurie become connected with the March family?
    Friendship and shared activities bring him into the sisters’ social world.
  7. Why does Amy destroy Jo’s manuscript?
    She acts in anger after being excluded, harming work that matters deeply to Jo.
  8. How does the skating emergency change Jo and Amy’s conflict?
    Danger shifts attention toward their relationship and the need to respond to hurt constructively.
  9. What does Meg’s experience at a fashionable gathering reveal?
    Wealth and social expectations complicate her sense of belonging and self-worth.
  10. How does Beth’s illness affect the household?
    The family reorganizes around care and confronts uncertainty and fear.
  11. Why does Jo reject Laurie’s proposal?
    She does not see their relationship as the marriage he wants, despite deep affection.
  12. What does Jo’s move away make possible?
    She gains new experiences, independence, and opportunities to write.
  13. How does Amy’s interest in art connect with questions about opportunity?
    Training, travel, and social resources influence the future she can pursue.
  14. What does Beth’s later decline change for Jo?
    Loss leads Jo to reconsider memory, family, and the purpose of her writing.
  15. How does Jo’s later writing differ from work aimed only at sensational appeal?
    It draws on experiences and relationships that matter to her.
  16. What possibilities does the ending create at Plumfield?
    A school and new relationships connect creative work with community life.
  17. Compare the ambitions of two sisters using scenes.
    Accept distinct goals and constraints rather than ranking one life as automatically superior.
  18. How does the film show family care without making all conflict disappear?
    Accept a scene where loyalty coexists with disagreement, regret, or loss.
  19. What would you examine when comparing a novel passage with this adaptation?
    Whose viewpoint is emphasized, what is omitted, and how visual choices affect meaning.
  20. How does Jo’s authorship help shape the meaning of the family story?
    Writing preserves experience while also selecting and interpreting it.

Final writing: Look for a clear claim, two accurately described scenes, reasoning, and a meaningful limitation of the portrayal or a question for further study. Accept different supported interpretations. No graphic descriptions are required.
