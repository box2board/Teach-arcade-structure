Teaching Notes

Learning goals & preparation

Essential question: How does Flags of Our Fathers reveal the gap between a public image of heroism and individual experience?

  • Track character choices and the pressures shaping them.
  • Support an interpretation with accurately described scenes.
  • Distinguish film portrayal from historical evidence and identify a source to investigate.

Locate Iwo Jima and place February 1945 within the Pacific War. Introduce war bonds, propaganda, flashback, oral history, and survivor guilt. Explain that the film moves across time; students should track settings and speakers rather than assume chronological order. Explain Ira Hayes’s Pima identity without treating Indigenous people as one culture.

Content considerations & viewing plan
  • Graphic combat injuries, deaths, bodies, explosions, and disturbing descriptions of wartime harm.
  • Strong language, grief, survivor guilt, and alcohol misuse.
  • Racist treatment of Ira Hayes, a Pima Marine, and demeaning language; discuss without requiring students to repeat slurs.

Preview the assigned edition and arrange required permissions and an alternative source-based assignment. Students can explain harm without describing graphic acts or repeating slurs. Do not require personal disclosures or reenactments of persecution.

Pause after the landing and flag-raising sequences, during the bond tour’s reenactment, after conflicts over public hero status, and after the later recollections. Because flashbacks recur, use narrative events rather than invented minute marks.

Use brief responses during viewing and reserve interpretation for afterward. Check subtitles and captions. If scenes are omitted, bridge the narrative and adapt the affected questions.

Historical context & source comparison

The film reflects identifications accepted when it was made. The Marine Corps later identified Harold Schultz rather than John Bradley in the famous second-raising photograph (2016), and Harold Keller rather than Rene Gagnon (2019). Questions about Doc and Rene concern the film’s portrayal, not the current identification. The photographed second raising replaced a smaller flag; it was not a staged fake of a battle already over. Compare the 2019 Marine Corps statement with the film and ask how new evidence revises public memory.

For a short comparison, select one accessible source or excerpt, establish author/date/purpose, and compare one film claim with what it supports and leaves unanswered. Teacher selection and adaptation are required.

Discussion & optional extensions
  • What does the photograph show, and what remains outside its frame?
  • Why might public praise feel uncomfortable to someone remembering friends who died?
  • How should an interpretation change when the people in an image are reidentified?

Exit ticket: One claim, one supporting scene, and one historical question.

Optional extension: Build an evidence chart with columns for film portrayal, external source, agreement or difference, and remaining uncertainty. Assess accuracy and reasoning.

Paper Worksheet Answer Key

Expected answers for 13 questions. Accept supported alternatives for analytical prompts.

  1. What can a photograph show about an event, and what might it leave out?
    It captures a selected moment; it cannot alone establish the full sequence, all participants, motives, or later consequences.
  2. How does the landing sequence challenge an easy picture of victory?
    Danger, confusion, casualties, and uncertainty foreground the costs of the battle rather than a simple triumph.
  3. What role does Doc Bradley play among the Marines in the film?
    He is a Navy corpsman caring for wounded Marines. This concerns his film role, not the revised identification in the photo.
  4. Why is a second, larger flag raised on Mount Suribachi?
    It replaces the smaller flag already raised. The second raising, photographed by Rosenthal, is distinct from the first.
  5. Why does the flag photograph become valuable to officials at home?
    It offers a memorable hopeful symbol that can mobilize public support and help sell war bonds.
  6. Which three men does the film send on the bond tour?
    The film follows John “Doc” Bradley, Rene Gagnon, and Ira Hayes. Later research revised Bradley’s and Gagnon’s identification in the photograph.
  7. How does the stadium reenactment differ from the battle experience?
    It packages the flag raising as organized spectacle, while memories show danger, losses, and unresolved distress.
  8. Why is Ira uncomfortable with the public hero narrative?
    He remembers comrades who died and resists having the pictured men singled out as the entire story of courage.
  9. How does the film show racism affecting Ira during the tour?
    He faces racial exclusion or demeaning treatment despite public praise as a war hero. Describe an example without repeating slurs.
  10. How do flashbacks interrupt the apparent celebration?
    Memories of combat and loss intrude on tour scenes, contrasting the public event with private experience.
  11. Choose one scene that contrasts public celebration with private memory. Explain it.
    Accept a specific paired contrast, such as the reenactment and flashbacks, with reasoning about what each perspective reveals.
  12. What must we distinguish when discussing Doc or Rene as flag raisers today?
    Distinguish the film’s 2006 portrayal from the Marine Corps corrections identifying Schultz and Keller in the famous photograph.
  13. How could you investigate a detail that a famous photograph cannot explain?
    Use caption history, contemporaneous records, additional photographs, or oral histories; compare evidence and its limitations.
Online Worksheet Answer Key

Expected answers for 20 questions. Accept supported alternatives for analytical prompts.

  1. What can a photograph show about an event, and what might it leave out?
    It captures a selected moment; it cannot alone establish the full sequence, all participants, motives, or later consequences.
  2. Why would a government ask civilians to buy war bonds?
    Bonds raise funds for the war and encourage civilian participation and support.
  3. How does the landing sequence challenge an easy picture of victory?
    Danger, confusion, casualties, and uncertainty foreground the costs of the battle rather than a simple triumph.
  4. What role does Doc Bradley play among the Marines in the film?

  7. Which three men does the film send on the bond tour?
    The film follows John “Doc” Bradley, Rene Gagnon, and Ira Hayes. Later research revised Bradley’s and Gagnon’s identification in the photograph.
  8. What are the touring men expected to do for the war effort?
    Make public appearances, tell an appealing story, and promote bond purchases using their association with the photograph.
  9. How does the stadium reenactment differ from the battle experience?
    It packages the flag raising as organized spectacle, while memories show danger, losses, and unresolved distress.
  10. Why is Ira uncomfortable with the public hero narrative?
    He remembers comrades who died and resists having the pictured men singled out as the entire story of courage.
  11. How does the film show racism affecting Ira during the tour?
    He faces racial exclusion or demeaning treatment despite public praise as a war hero. Describe an example without repeating slurs.
  12. How do flashbacks interrupt the apparent celebration?
    Memories of combat and loss intrude on tour scenes, contrasting the public event with private experience.
  13. How do the men’s responses to attention differ?
    Rene is more drawn to opportunity, Ira struggles visibly, and Doc is quieter and burdened by memory. Support a comparison with scenes.
  14. What happens when the tour no longer meets officials’ expectations?
    Public usefulness affects how the men are treated; Ira is sent back, exposing the conditional nature of official celebration.
  15. How does the film portray the lasting effect of losing comrades?
    Memories, grief, silence, and difficulty returning to civilian life show that consequences extend beyond the battle.
  16. Why are later interviews and recollections important to the story?
    They recover experiences missing from the public photograph and show how family and others reconstruct memory.
  17. Choose one scene that contrasts public celebration with private memory. Explain it.
    Accept a specific paired contrast, such as the reenactment and flashbacks, with reasoning about what each perspective reveals.
  18. What definition of heroism does the film invite viewers to consider?
    Accept an evidence-based interpretation emphasizing comradeship, ordinary service, care, or sacrifice beyond fame.
  19. What must we distinguish when discussing Doc or Rene as flag raisers today?
    Distinguish the film’s 2006 portrayal from the Marine Corps corrections identifying Schultz and Keller in the famous photograph.
  20. How could you investigate a detail that a famous photograph cannot explain?
    Use caption history, contemporaneous records, additional photographs, or oral histories; compare evidence and its limitations.

Final writing: Look for a clear claim, two accurately described scenes, reasoning, and a meaningful limitation of film as historical evidence. Accept different supported interpretations. No graphic descriptions are required.
