Teaching Notes

Learning goals & preparation

Essential question: How can we recognize common experiences without erasing important differences?

  • Distinguish filmed observations and participant accounts from interpretation.
  • Support an analysis with two accurately described moments from this documentary.
  • Identify how selection, editing, or missing perspectives limit a conclusion.

Introduce perspective, context, translation, and selection. The film premiered in 2011 and had a later U.S. theatrical release in 2014. Preview discussions of suicide and bullying. Students may analyze interviewees without sharing personal experiences.

Content considerations & viewing plan
  • Children discuss bullying, violence, hardship, and suicide-related distress.
  • Family separation, grief, and discussion of war and terrorism.
  • Some mature language and topics; offer an alternative without requiring explanation.

Preview the assigned edition and arrange required permissions and an alternative source-based assignment. Students can explain harm without describing graphic acts or repeating slurs. Keep discussion focused on participants; do not require personal disclosures.

Pause after contrasting home settings, after two interviews on a shared topic, after a discussion of difficult experiences, and before the final reflections.

Use brief responses during viewing and reserve interpretation for afterward. Check subtitles and captions. If scenes are omitted, bridge the narrative and adapt the affected questions.

Context & source comparison

Fifteen countries and selected children cannot represent all childhood experiences. Identify individual statements rather than assigning them to a whole nation. Consider how questions, translation, and editing influence what viewers understand.

For a short comparison, select one accessible source or excerpt, establish author/date/purpose, and compare one film claim with what it supports and leaves unanswered. Teacher selection and adaptation are required.

Discussion & optional extensions
  • How can we recognize common experiences without erasing important differences?
  • Which scene best supports your interpretation, and what might another viewer notice?
  • What does the film leave out or simplify?

Exit ticket: One claim, one supporting scene, and one question for further study.

Optional extension: Build an evidence chart with columns for film portrayal, external source, agreement or difference, and remaining uncertainty. Assess accuracy and reasoning.

Paper Worksheet Answer Key

Expected answers for 13 questions. Accept supported alternatives for analytical prompts.

  1. How does context help us understand a person’s statement?
    Family, place, language, and circumstances can affect meaning without determining every belief.
  2. Choose an interviewee and identify a detail of daily life.
    Accurately describe the detail and identify the child or setting as the film presents it.
  3. What does a home or community scene add to that interview?
    Explain a visible circumstance rather than making an unsupported assumption.
  4. How does another child describe a similar part of life differently?
    Compare two specific statements or scenes without ranking their lives.
  5. What important difference remains within that connection?
    Explain a specific difference in context or experience supported by the film.
  6. How does one child discuss family or belonging?
    Summarize the individual’s account without generalizing to all children in the setting.
  7. How does another child describe a hope for the future?
    Accurately identify the hope and the perspective it reveals.
  8. How can a serious topic appear alongside humor or ordinary interests?
    Different parts of life coexist; one difficult account does not define the whole child.
  9. What do subtitles or translation contribute to access?
    They allow viewers to understand speech while mediating its phrasing and meaning.
  10. What does an image of a setting establish, and what does it not?
    It shows selected visible conditions but not every aspect of a person’s life.
  11. Identify a commonality without erasing a difference.
    Support both parts with two accurately described interviews.
  12. What broader claim would need outside evidence?
    Choose a claim about schooling, living conditions, or beliefs and identify an appropriate source.
  13. What is a careful conclusion about the children shown?
    Describe a supported pattern while noting that selection and individual variation limit generalization.
Online Worksheet Answer Key

Expected answers for 20 questions. Accept supported alternatives for analytical prompts.

  1. How does context help us understand a person’s statement?
    Family, place, language, and circumstances can affect meaning without determining every belief.
  2. Why is one interview not evidence about an entire country?
    It represents one selected individual rather than a complete or random national sample.
  3. Choose an interviewee and identify a detail of daily life.
    Accurately describe the detail and identify the child or setting as the film presents it.
  4. What does a home or community scene add to that interview?
    Explain a visible circumstance rather than making an unsupported assumption.
  5. How does another child describe a similar part of life differently?
    Compare two specific statements or scenes without ranking their lives.
  6. What interest or activity creates a connection across settings?
    Identify a shared interest shown by at least two interviewees.
  7. What important difference remains within that connection?
    Explain a specific difference in context or experience supported by the film.
  8. How does one child discuss family or belonging?
    Summarize the individual’s account without generalizing to all children in the setting.
  9. How does another child describe a hope for the future?
    Accurately identify the hope and the perspective it reveals.
  10. What concern does one interviewee raise about the wider world?
    Name the concern and distinguish the child’s view from a verified global claim.
  11. How can a serious topic appear alongside humor or ordinary interests?
    Different parts of life coexist; one difficult account does not define the whole child.
  12. How does comparing two answers complicate an early assumption?
    Describe what the viewer might initially think and the additional evidence.
  13. What do subtitles or translation contribute to access?
    They allow viewers to understand speech while mediating its phrasing and meaning.
  14. How can the interviewer’s question shape an answer?
    Its wording and topic influence what the child is invited to discuss.
  15. How does editing connect separate interviews?
    Placement can emphasize common themes or contrasts that viewers may read as an argument.
  16. What does an image of a setting establish, and what does it not?
    It shows selected visible conditions but not every aspect of a person’s life.
  17. Identify a commonality without erasing a difference.
    Support both parts with two accurately described interviews.
  18. What viewpoint seems less visible in this selected portrait?
    Name a perspective or circumstance whose absence limits a conclusion.
  19. What broader claim would need outside evidence?
    Choose a claim about schooling, living conditions, or beliefs and identify an appropriate source.
  20. What is a careful conclusion about the children shown?
    Describe a supported pattern while noting that selection and individual variation limit generalization.

Final writing: Look for a clear claim, two accurately described scenes, reasoning, and a meaningful limitation of the portrayal or a question for further study. Accept different supported interpretations. No graphic descriptions are required.
