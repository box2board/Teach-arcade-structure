Teaching Notes

Learning goals & preparation

Essential question: How do modern film choices change the way we interpret Shakespeare’s tragedy?

  • Analyze the relationship between retained language and modern imagery.
  • Track how secrecy, violence, and failed communication drive the plot.
  • Compare one scene and its ending details with the play.

Use the 1996 Baz Luhrmann film, not the 1968 or 2013 adaptations. Introduce feud, dramatic irony, prologue, imagery, and tragedy. Provide selected play scenes with vocabulary support. Preview the death sequences and make an alternative text-and-still-image task available.

Content considerations & viewing plan
  • Gun violence, killings, blood, threatening confrontations, and the lovers’ deaths by suicide.
  • Sexual references, a brief intimate scene, partial nudity, and intense kissing.
  • Drug use, smoking, grief, and harsh parental pressure.

Preview the assigned edition and arrange required permissions and an alternative source-based assignment. Students can explain harm without describing graphic acts or repeating slurs. Keep discussion focused on characters; do not require personal disclosures.

Pause after the party and meeting, after the private marriage, after the street conflict and banishment, and before the final sequence according to your previewed plan.

Use brief responses during viewing and reserve interpretation for afterward. Check subtitles and captions. If scenes are omitted, bridge the narrative and adapt the affected questions.

Context & source comparison

The film cuts and rearranges Shakespeare’s language and changes staging and the final death sequence. Compare a selected scene with the Folger text rather than calling the dialogue a complete unaltered script. Discuss suicide as a tragic outcome, not a proof of love, and do not ask students to describe methods or disclose personal experiences.

For a short comparison, select one accessible source or excerpt, establish author/date/purpose, and compare one film claim with what it supports and leaves unanswered. Teacher selection and adaptation are required.

Studio credits and rating · Content advisory (spoilers) · Folger: complete play

Discussion & optional extensions
  • How do modern film choices change the way we interpret Shakespeare’s tragedy?
  • Which scene best supports your interpretation, and what might another viewer notice?
  • What does the film leave out or simplify?

Exit ticket: One claim, one supporting scene, and one question for further study.

Optional extension: Build an evidence chart with columns for film portrayal, external source, agreement or difference, and remaining uncertainty. Assess accuracy and reasoning.

Paper Worksheet Answer Key

Expected answers for 13 questions. Accept supported alternatives for analytical prompts.

  1. How can a modern setting change the effect of old dialogue?
    Costumes, objects, music, and pacing can give familiar words new associations and emphasis.
  2. How does the televised prologue frame the feud?
    News presentation makes the conflict appear public, recurring, and familiar to the city.
  3. What do the opening confrontation and weapons suggest about the families’ rivalry?
    It is dangerous and socially visible rather than confined to a private disagreement.
  4. How does Romeo appear before meeting Juliet?
    He is preoccupied with romantic disappointment and speaks about love before encountering her.
  5. Why does learning each other’s family name matter?
    Their attraction crosses a feud that makes the relationship dangerous and difficult to reveal.
  6. How does the courtyard and pool scene adapt the private conversation?
    It keeps intimate dialogue but changes space and movement, using concealment and water in place of conventional balcony staging.
  7. Why does Friar Laurence agree to marry the couple?
    He hopes the marriage might help reconcile the families, despite the risks of haste and secrecy.
  8. Why does Romeo initially resist fighting Tybalt?
    His secret marriage makes Tybalt family, though the others do not know that relationship.
  9. What pressure does Juliet face concerning Paris?
    Her family expects her to marry him and responds harshly to her resistance.
  10. What does the final television framing add to the ending?
    It returns private loss to public spectacle and asks viewers to consider the continuing consequences of the feud.
  11. Choose a modern image or sound that changes a scene’s meaning.
    Accept a precise choice and interpretation grounded in the scene, not merely a claim that it looks modern.
  12. What should you compare in the film and the play’s final scene?
    Timing, what the lovers perceive, actions, and emotional emphasis; note that the film changes the sequence.
  13. Does the ending invite criticism of more than the lovers’ decisions?
    Accept supported analysis of family conflict, authority, violence, or failed support; avoid romanticizing suicide.
Online Worksheet Answer Key

Expected answers for 20 questions. Accept supported alternatives for analytical prompts.

  1. How can a modern setting change the effect of old dialogue?
    Costumes, objects, music, and pacing can give familiar words new associations and emphasis.
  2. What is dramatic irony?
    The audience knows something important that a character does not, affecting how it interprets a scene.
  3. How does the televised prologue frame the feud?
    News presentation makes the conflict appear public, recurring, and familiar to the city.
  4. What do the opening confrontation and weapons suggest about the families’ rivalry?
    It is dangerous and socially visible rather than confined to a private disagreement.
  5. How does Romeo appear before meeting Juliet?
    He is preoccupied with romantic disappointment and speaks about love before encountering her.
  6. How do costumes and the aquarium shape the lovers’ first meeting?
    Visual separation and a quiet shared gaze contrast with the crowded party; accept a supported symbolic reading.
  7. Why does learning each other’s family name matter?
    Their attraction crosses a feud that makes the relationship dangerous and difficult to reveal.
  8. How does the courtyard and pool scene adapt the private conversation?
    It keeps intimate dialogue but changes space and movement, using concealment and water in place of conventional balcony staging.
  9. Why does Friar Laurence agree to marry the couple?
    He hopes the marriage might help reconcile the families, despite the risks of haste and secrecy.
  10. How does secrecy complicate the lovers’ choices?
    Important adults and friends lack information, limiting support and increasing misunderstanding.
  11. Why does Romeo initially resist fighting Tybalt?
    His secret marriage makes Tybalt family, though the others do not know that relationship.
  12. How do Mercutio’s death and Romeo’s response change the conflict?
    Romeo retaliates against Tybalt, turning an already dangerous feud into further killing and banishment.
  13. What pressure does Juliet face concerning Paris?
    Her family expects her to marry him and responds harshly to her resistance.
  14. Why does Juliet turn to Friar Laurence for another plan?
    Her secret marriage and the imposed wedding leave her seeking help outside her parents’ knowledge.
  15. How does failed communication create dramatic irony near the end?
    Romeo receives misleading news of Juliet’s death without the explanation of the plan that viewers know.
  16. What does the final television framing add to the ending?
    It returns private loss to public spectacle and asks viewers to consider the continuing consequences of the feud.
  17. Choose a modern image or sound that changes a scene’s meaning.
    Accept a precise choice and interpretation grounded in the scene, not merely a claim that it looks modern.
  18. How do haste and secrecy interact with the feud?
    Limited time and missing information magnify the harm of existing conflict; cite two connected moments.
  19. What should you compare in the film and the play’s final scene?
    Timing, what the lovers perceive, actions, and emotional emphasis; note that the film changes the sequence.
  20. Does the ending invite criticism of more than the lovers’ decisions?
    Accept supported analysis of family conflict, authority, violence, or failed support; avoid romanticizing suicide.

Final writing: Look for a clear claim, two accurately described scenes, reasoning, and a meaningful limitation of the portrayal or a question for further study. Accept different supported interpretations. No graphic descriptions are required.
