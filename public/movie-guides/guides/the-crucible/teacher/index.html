Teaching Notes

Learning goals & preparation

Essential question: How does fear turn an accusation into authority, and what can resist that process?

  • Explain how accusation & evidence shapes character choices.
  • Support an interpretation with two accurately described scenes.
  • Compare the portrayal with a selected passage from its source or another relevant text.

Introduce allegation, testimony, coercion, dramatic irony, and allegory. Use the 1996 film. Discuss the play’s 1950s context alongside Salem’s 1692 events; neither the play nor this film is a documentary.

Content considerations & viewing plan
  • Brief nudity and sexual references; an affair is central to the conflict.
  • Threats, coercion, imprisonment, hanging and crushing deaths, and brief bloody animal harm.
  • Religious persecution, false accusations, and some language.

Preview the assigned edition and arrange required permissions and an alternative source-based assignment. Students can explain harm without describing graphic acts or repeating slurs. Keep discussion focused on characters; do not require personal disclosures.

Pause after the first accusations, after Elizabeth’s arrest, during the court confrontation, and before the final decision.

Use brief responses during viewing and reserve interpretation for afterward. Check subtitles and captions. If scenes are omitted, bridge the narrative and adapt the affected questions.

Context & source comparison

Miller alters ages and relationships and dramatizes private encounters. Compare a selected play passage with its film scene, then use a teacher-selected Salem record to separate dramatic invention from documented events. The opening woods sequence visualizes material largely reported in the play.

For a short comparison, select one accessible source or excerpt, establish author/date/purpose, and compare one film claim with what it supports and leaves unanswered. Teacher selection and adaptation are required.

AFI credits · Content advisory (spoilers)

Discussion & optional extensions
  • How does fear turn an accusation into authority, and what can resist that process?
  • Which scene best supports your interpretation, and what might another viewer notice?
  • What does the film leave out or simplify?

Exit ticket: One claim, one supporting scene, and one question for further study.

Optional extension: Build an evidence chart with columns for film portrayal, external source, agreement or difference, and remaining uncertainty. Assess accuracy and reasoning.

Paper Worksheet Answer Key

Expected answers for 13 questions. Accept supported alternatives for analytical prompts.

  1. What is the difference between an accusation and evidence?
    An accusation makes a claim; evidence supplies reasons that can be checked rather than assumed true.
  2. Why do the girls fear consequences after the woods gathering?
    They fear punishment for forbidden behavior and seek an explanation that shifts blame.
  3. Why is Parris concerned about the events in his household?
    He fears harm to his authority and standing as well as the condition of his daughter.
  4. How does Abigail’s relationship with Proctor complicate her motives?
    Their past affair and her desire for him affect her hostility toward Elizabeth.
  5. Why do more accusations follow the first confessions?
    The authorities reward confirmation of their assumptions, allowing fear and grudges to spread.
  6. What does the poppet become evidence of in the officials’ interpretation?
    They associate its needle with alleged harm to Abigail, treating an ordinary object as incriminating.
  7. Why is Elizabeth arrested?
    Abigail accuses her and the court accepts the poppet-related claim against her.
  8. Why does Proctor reveal his affair?
    He risks his reputation to expose Abigail’s motive and undermine her credibility.
  9. How do the girls use performance against Mary?
    They imitate her and claim supernatural harm, turning pressure back onto her.
  10. Why does Proctor reject the signed confession at the end?
    He refuses a public lie that would damage his integrity and validate the accusations.
  11. Choose a moment when reputation conflicts with truth.
    Accept Proctor’s admission, Elizabeth’s denial, or the court’s resistance, with accurate scene evidence.
  12. Why should the drama not serve as the only source on the Salem trials?
    It changes people and events for dramatic purposes and needs comparison with historical records.
  13. What would you compare in a play passage and its film scene?
    Dialogue, omissions, performance, setting, camera choices, and their effect on interpretation.
Online Worksheet Answer Key

Expected answers for 20 questions. Accept supported alternatives for analytical prompts.

  1. What is the difference between an accusation and evidence?
    An accusation makes a claim; evidence supplies reasons that can be checked rather than assumed true.
  2. How can concern about reputation influence a decision?
    A person may hide information or conform to avoid shame, even when honesty would be more useful.
  3. Why do the girls fear consequences after the woods gathering?
    They fear punishment for forbidden behavior and seek an explanation that shifts blame.
  4. Why is Parris concerned about the events in his household?
    He fears harm to his authority and standing as well as the condition of his daughter.
  5. How does Abigail’s relationship with Proctor complicate her motives?
    Their past affair and her desire for him affect her hostility toward Elizabeth.
  6. How does Tituba’s pressured confession change the situation?
    Naming others under pressure helps make accusation a route to safety and fuels further suspicion.
  7. Why do more accusations follow the first confessions?
    The authorities reward confirmation of their assumptions, allowing fear and grudges to spread.
  8. What does the poppet become evidence of in the officials’ interpretation?
    They associate its needle with alleged harm to Abigail, treating an ordinary object as incriminating.
  9. Why is Elizabeth arrested?
    Abigail accuses her and the court accepts the poppet-related claim against her.
  10. What does Mary Warren attempt to explain in court?
    She tries to acknowledge that the girls’ earlier performances and accusations were false.
  11. Why does Proctor reveal his affair?
    He risks his reputation to expose Abigail’s motive and undermine her credibility.
  12. How does Elizabeth’s denial create dramatic irony?
    She tries to protect Proctor without knowing he has already admitted the affair.
  13. How do the girls use performance against Mary?
    They imitate her and claim supernatural harm, turning pressure back onto her.
  14. What does Mary do when the pressure intensifies?
    She retracts her challenge and accuses Proctor, protecting herself at his expense.
  15. Why does the court resist evidence that would discredit earlier decisions?
    Admitting error could undermine its authority and expose harm already done.
  16. Why does Proctor reject the signed confession at the end?
    He refuses a public lie that would damage his integrity and validate the accusations.
  17. Choose a moment when reputation conflicts with truth.
    Accept Proctor’s admission, Elizabeth’s denial, or the court’s resistance, with accurate scene evidence.
  18. How does the film’s opening differ from a play that reports events through dialogue?
    Showing the gathering makes one interpretation visible; reported events can preserve different uncertainties.
  19. Why should the drama not serve as the only source on the Salem trials?
    It changes people and events for dramatic purposes and needs comparison with historical records.
  20. What would you compare in a play passage and its film scene?
    Dialogue, omissions, performance, setting, camera choices, and their effect on interpretation.

Final writing: Look for a clear claim, two accurately described scenes, reasoning, and a meaningful limitation of the portrayal or a question for further study. Accept different supported interpretations. No graphic descriptions are required.
