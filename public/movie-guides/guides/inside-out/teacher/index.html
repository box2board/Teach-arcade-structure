Teaching Notes

Learning goals & preparation

Essential question: How does acknowledging difficult feelings help Riley reconnect with others?

  • Distinguish an emotion from the action a character chooses.
  • Explain how listening changes Joy’s understanding of Sadness.
  • Analyze personification as a metaphor rather than literal brain science.

Use the original 2015 film. Introduce personification, mixed emotions, belonging, and supportive listening. This guide can support upper-elementary through secondary analysis; adjust writing expectations to the class. Let students discuss Riley and other characters instead of personal experiences. No disclosure of mental health or family experiences is required.

Content considerations & viewing plan
  • Homesickness, family arguments, loneliness, and a child attempting to run away.
  • Frightening imaginary scenes, peril, and the loss of a beloved imaginary character.
  • Some rude humor and emotionally intense moments of sadness.

Preview the assigned edition and arrange required permissions and an alternative source-based assignment. Students can explain harm without describing graphic acts or repeating slurs. Keep discussion focused on characters; do not require personal disclosures.

Pause after the move and first school day, after Joy and Sadness leave Headquarters, after Joy revisits the hockey memory, and after Riley returns home.

Use brief responses during viewing and reserve interpretation for afterward. Check subtitles and captions. If scenes are omitted, bridge the narrative and adapt the affected questions.

Context & source comparison

The control room, memory spheres, and personality islands are imaginative storytelling devices, not literal brain structures or a clinical explanation. The film selects five personified emotions rather than defining every feeling. Discuss supportive communication without diagnosing Riley, classmates, or family members. An alternative can analyze a fictional character’s response to a move.

For a short comparison, select one accessible source or excerpt, establish author/date/purpose, and compare one film claim with what it supports and leaves unanswered. Teacher selection and adaptation are required.

Discussion & optional extensions
  • How does acknowledging difficult feelings help Riley reconnect with others?
  • Which scene best supports your interpretation, and what might another viewer notice?
  • What does the film leave out or simplify?

Exit ticket: One claim, one supporting scene, and one question for further study.

Optional extension: Build an evidence chart with columns for film portrayal, external source, agreement or difference, and remaining uncertainty. Assess accuracy and reasoning.

Paper Worksheet Answer Key

Expected answers for 13 questions. Accept supported alternatives for analytical prompts.

  1. How is having a feeling different from choosing an action?
    A feeling is an experience; people can consider different responses rather than treating an emotion as an order.
  2. Which five emotions are personified in Riley’s Headquarters?
    Joy, Sadness, Fear, Anger, and Disgust; the film is not a complete list of human emotions.
  3. What changes in Riley’s life when her family moves?
    Her home, school, friendships, hockey routine, and familiar surroundings change.
  4. How does Joy initially treat Sadness?
    She tries to limit or exclude her influence, assuming that keeping Riley happy is the main goal.
  5. How do Joy and Sadness become separated from Headquarters?
    A struggle involving the core memories sends them out with the memories, leaving the other emotions in control.
  6. How do the personality islands represent Riley’s changing situation?
    They visualize valued parts of her life becoming unstable as connections and routines are disrupted.
  7. Why is Bing Bong important to the journey?
    He is Riley’s fading imaginary friend and helps Joy and Sadness navigate the memory world.
  8. How does Sadness comforting Bing Bong change Joy’s understanding?
    It shows that recognizing sadness can create connection and comfort rather than only making things worse.
  9. What happens to Bing Bong during the attempt to leave the memory dump?
    He sacrifices his chance to return so Joy can escape; describe the loss without requiring personal comparisons.
  10. How do Riley’s parents respond when she shares her feelings?
    They listen, acknowledge missing Minnesota too, and comfort her rather than demanding constant happiness.
  11. Choose a scene showing that comfort is different from simply cheering someone up.
    Accept Sadness listening to Bing Bong or the family reunion, with an explanation of recognition and support.
  12. Why should the memory spheres not be described as literal brain science?
    They are visual metaphors created for a story, not physical memory containers or a complete model.
  13. What supportive response could a fictional friend offer Riley during the move?
    Listen, acknowledge the change, invite inclusion, or help her seek a trusted adult; no personal disclosure is required.
Online Worksheet Answer Key

Expected answers for 20 questions. Accept supported alternatives for analytical prompts.

  1. How is having a feeling different from choosing an action?
    A feeling is an experience; people can consider different responses rather than treating an emotion as an order.
  2. What does personification let a storyteller do?
    It gives an abstract idea human-like behavior so viewers can follow conflicts and relationships.
  3. Which five emotions are personified in Riley’s Headquarters?
    Joy, Sadness, Fear, Anger, and Disgust; the film is not a complete list of human emotions.
  4. What changes in Riley’s life when her family moves?
    Her home, school, friendships, hockey routine, and familiar surroundings change.
  5. How does Joy initially treat Sadness?
    She tries to limit or exclude her influence, assuming that keeping Riley happy is the main goal.
  6. What happens when Riley speaks about Minnesota at school?
    A familiar memory becomes sad and she cries, showing how the move affects her feelings.
  7. How do Joy and Sadness become separated from Headquarters?
    A struggle involving the core memories sends them out with the memories, leaving the other emotions in control.
  8. How do the personality islands represent Riley’s changing situation?
    They visualize valued parts of her life becoming unstable as connections and routines are disrupted.
  9. Why is Bing Bong important to the journey?
    He is Riley’s fading imaginary friend and helps Joy and Sadness navigate the memory world.
  10. How does Sadness respond when Bing Bong is upset?
    She listens and acknowledges the loss instead of immediately trying to distract him.
  11. How does Sadness comforting Bing Bong change Joy’s understanding?
    It shows that recognizing sadness can create connection and comfort rather than only making things worse.
  12. What does Joy learn by revisiting the hockey memory?
    A sad moment brought family and teammates’ support, helping her see sadness’s role in connection.
  13. What happens to Bing Bong during the attempt to leave the memory dump?
    He sacrifices his chance to return so Joy can escape; describe the loss without requiring personal comparisons.
  14. Why is Riley’s plan to return to Minnesota dangerous?
    She leaves without support or a safe plan, confusing recovery of the old setting with solving her distress.
  15. What becomes possible when Sadness returns to the controls?
    Riley can recognize and express what she misses, abandon the runaway plan, and seek her parents.
  16. How do Riley’s parents respond when she shares her feelings?
    They listen, acknowledge missing Minnesota too, and comfort her rather than demanding constant happiness.
  17. Choose a scene showing that comfort is different from simply cheering someone up.
    Accept Sadness listening to Bing Bong or the family reunion, with an explanation of recognition and support.
  18. What do the mixed-color memories suggest?
    An experience can hold more than one feeling, complicating the idea that memories must be purely happy or sad.
  19. Why should the memory spheres not be described as literal brain science?
    They are visual metaphors created for a story, not physical memory containers or a complete model.
  20. What supportive response could a fictional friend offer Riley during the move?
    Listen, acknowledge the change, invite inclusion, or help her seek a trusted adult; no personal disclosure is required.

Final writing: Look for a clear claim, two accurately described scenes, reasoning, and a meaningful limitation of the portrayal or a question for further study. Accept different supported interpretations. No graphic descriptions are required.
