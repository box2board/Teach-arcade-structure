Teaching Notes

Learning goals & preparation

Essential question: How does the film use memory to explain conflict over adapting a story?

  • Explain how authorship shapes character choices.
  • Support an interpretation with two accurately described scenes.
  • Compare the portrayal with an appropriate external source and identify what remains uncertain.

Preview the childhood distress and parental crisis scenes. Introduce adaptation, authorship, flashback, and biographical dramatization. Pair a selected Mary Poppins passage or a teacher-provided historical source with the film.

Content considerations & viewing plan
  • Alcohol misuse, parental illness and death, and intense childhood distress.
  • A mother’s apparent suicide attempt involving water; adult themes and some language.
  • Grief and family instability; do not require personal disclosure or treat one childhood event as a complete explanation of a person.

Preview the assigned edition and arrange required permissions and an alternative source-based assignment. Students can explain harm without describing graphic acts or repeating slurs. Keep discussion focused on characters; do not require personal disclosures.

Pause after the first script meeting, after a childhood flashback, during debate over Mr. Banks, and after the premiere.

Use brief responses during viewing and reserve interpretation for afterward. Check subtitles and captions. If scenes are omitted, bridge the narrative and adapt the affected questions.

Context & source comparison

The film is a Disney-produced interpretation of negotiations involving P. L. Travers and Walt Disney, not a complete record of their relationship or Travers’s views. Its imagined private conversations and emotional resolution require comparison with independent sources.

For a short comparison, select one accessible source or excerpt, establish author/date/purpose, and compare one film claim with what it supports and leaves unanswered. Teacher selection and adaptation are required.

Studio film information · Content advisory (spoilers)

Discussion & optional extensions
  • How does the film use memory to explain conflict over adapting a story?
  • Which scene best supports your interpretation, and what might another viewer notice?
  • What does the film leave out or simplify?

Exit ticket: One claim, one supporting scene, and one question for further study.

Optional extension: Build an evidence chart with columns for film portrayal, external source, agreement or difference, and remaining uncertainty. Assess accuracy and reasoning.

Paper Worksheet Answer Key

Expected answers for 13 questions. Accept supported alternatives for analytical prompts.

  1. What questions arise when someone else adapts an author’s work?
    Who controls changes, what must be preserved, and how a new medium alters meaning are central questions.
  2. Why is Travers reluctant to approve the adaptation?
    She worries that changes will distort her characters and the meaning of her work.
  3. How do the studio surroundings contrast with her expectations?
    Their cheerful commercial world clashes with her guarded approach to authorship.
  4. What do her objections reveal about control?
    Details such as tone, design, and music become ways to protect her interpretation of the story.
  5. What does the first childhood material establish?
    It introduces family relationships and circumstances that the film later connects with her writing.
  6. How do the Sherman brothers’ songs change the meetings?
    Music provides another way to communicate ideas and sometimes creates moments of connection.
  7. Why can enjoying a song coexist with objecting to an adaptation?
    A person can value one creative choice without approving every alteration or losing all concerns.
  8. How does the mother’s distress complicate the childhood story?
    It reveals pressures affecting the household rather than presenting the father’s experience alone.
  9. How does Walt’s personal account function in the negotiation?
    The film uses shared vulnerability to support its argument about understanding and storytelling.
  10. Why does that response not establish complete historical agreement?
    A depicted emotional moment cannot prove that all objections or later disagreements disappeared.
  11. Which conflict illustrates a meaningful tradeoff in adaptation?
    Describe one specific disagreement and explain what each side wants to preserve or change.
  12. What source could test one claim about the negotiations?
    A contemporaneous recording, letter, or independent archival account could check a specific depicted event.
  13. How might the producing studio’s perspective affect the story?
    Its selection of conflicts and resolution can favor a particular account; identify evidence rather than assuming all scenes are false.
Online Worksheet Answer Key

Expected answers for 20 questions. Accept supported alternatives for analytical prompts.

  1. What questions arise when someone else adapts an author’s work?
    Who controls changes, what must be preserved, and how a new medium alters meaning are central questions.
  2. What can a flashback suggest without proving?
    It can offer an interpretation of motives, but a dramatized connection is not independent historical evidence.
  3. Why is Travers reluctant to approve the adaptation?
    She worries that changes will distort her characters and the meaning of her work.
  4. How do the studio surroundings contrast with her expectations?
    Their cheerful commercial world clashes with her guarded approach to authorship.
  5. What do her objections reveal about control?
    Details such as tone, design, and music become ways to protect her interpretation of the story.
  6. How do the filmmakers respond to her resistance?
    They negotiate, explain choices, and sometimes clash as they try to move the adaptation forward.
  7. What does the first childhood material establish?
    It introduces family relationships and circumstances that the film later connects with her writing.
  8. How do the Sherman brothers’ songs change the meetings?
    Music provides another way to communicate ideas and sometimes creates moments of connection.
  9. Why can enjoying a song coexist with objecting to an adaptation?
    A person can value one creative choice without approving every alteration or losing all concerns.
  10. How does the film connect the father in the flashbacks with Mr. Banks?
    It invites a comparison between a remembered parent and a character whose treatment matters deeply to Travers.
  11. How does the mother’s distress complicate the childhood story?
    It reveals pressures affecting the household rather than presenting the father’s experience alone.
  12. Why should viewers question a single explanation for Travers’s motives?
    Her professional, artistic, and personal life cannot be fully explained by selected childhood scenes.
  13. How does Walt’s personal account function in the negotiation?
    The film uses shared vulnerability to support its argument about understanding and storytelling.
  14. What does Travers seek in the treatment of Mr. Banks?
    She wants his dignity and complexity preserved rather than allowing him to become merely a comic obstacle.
  15. How does the premiere provide an emotional resolution in the film?
    Her response lets the film suggest a changed relationship to memory and the adaptation.
  16. Why does that response not establish complete historical agreement?
    A depicted emotional moment cannot prove that all objections or later disagreements disappeared.
  17. Which conflict illustrates a meaningful tradeoff in adaptation?
    Describe one specific disagreement and explain what each side wants to preserve or change.
  18. How does the order of present-day and childhood scenes shape interpretation?
    Explain how a flashback invites the viewer to connect memory with a later decision.
  19. What source could test one claim about the negotiations?
    A contemporaneous recording, letter, or independent archival account could check a specific depicted event.
  20. How might the producing studio’s perspective affect the story?
    Its selection of conflicts and resolution can favor a particular account; identify evidence rather than assuming all scenes are false.

Final writing: Look for a clear claim, two accurately described scenes, reasoning, and a meaningful limitation of the portrayal or a question for further study. Accept different supported interpretations. No graphic descriptions are required.
