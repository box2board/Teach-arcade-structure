Teaching Notes

Learning goals & preparation

Essential question: How do expertise, advocacy, and collaboration change access to opportunity?

  • Compare the different barriers and strategies facing the three women.
  • Explain how human expertise supports technological change.
  • Check the film’s compressed chronology against NASA biographies.

Introduce segregation, NACA versus NASA, human computers, trajectory, orbital flight, and FORTRAN. Distinguish John Glenn’s orbital mission from the later Moon landing. Give students a three-person tracking chart; no advanced calculus is needed.

Content considerations & viewing plan
  • Racial and gender discrimination, humiliating exclusion, tense arguments, and period terminology.
  • Some profanity, brief romantic scenes, and adult drinking.
  • Spaceflight danger and family or workplace stress.

Preview the assigned edition and arrange required permissions and an alternative source-based assignment. Students can explain harm without describing graphic acts or repeating slurs. Keep discussion focused on characters; do not require personal disclosures.

Pause after the workplace assignments, during the education and programming efforts, before Glenn’s launch, and after the mission outcome.

Use brief responses during viewing and reserve interpretation for afterward. Check subtitles and captions. If scenes are omitted, bridge the narrative and adapt the affected questions.

Context & source comparison

NASA’s biographies place Vaughan’s supervisory promotion in 1949 and Jackson’s engineering qualification in 1958, before the film’s main 1961–1962 setting. The film compresses chronology and uses composite characters and dramatized interventions. Compare one career milestone with a NASA biography. Individual achievement does not mean systemic barriers vanished.

For a short comparison, select one accessible source or excerpt, establish author/date/purpose, and compare one film claim with what it supports and leaves unanswered. Teacher selection and adaptation are required.

Studio details · Content advisory (spoilers) · NASA: Dorothy Vaughan · NASA: Mary Jackson · NASA: Katherine Johnson

Discussion & optional extensions
  • How do expertise, advocacy, and collaboration change access to opportunity?
  • Which scene best supports your interpretation, and what might another viewer notice?
  • What does the film leave out or simplify?

Exit ticket: One claim, one supporting scene, and one question for further study.

Optional extension: Build an evidence chart with columns for film portrayal, external source, agreement or difference, and remaining uncertainty. Assess accuracy and reasoning.

Paper Worksheet Answer Key

Expected answers for 13 questions. Accept supported alternatives for analytical prompts.

  1. How can a workplace rule block opportunity even when someone has the needed skill?
    Rules about education, promotion, access, or facilities can exclude qualified people; ability alone does not remove a barrier.
  2. What kind of work do the women initially perform at Langley?
    They perform mathematical calculations as human computers supporting aeronautical and space research.
  3. What assignment brings Katherine into the Space Task Group?
    Her mathematical skills lead to an assignment calculating and checking flight-related work for the group.
  4. Name one barrier Katherine faces in her new workplace.
    Accept restricted information, exclusion from meetings, unequal facilities, or dismissal of her expertise, with a scene.
  5. Why does Mary need additional classes to pursue engineering?
    The position’s qualifications require courses whose access is restricted by segregation.
  6. What does Katherine’s explanation of her long absences reveal?
    Segregated bathroom access costs time and imposes an unequal burden; this scene is a dramatization.
  7. Why does Dorothy decide to learn FORTRAN?
    She anticipates electronic computing and prepares herself and her coworkers for new kinds of work.
  8. How does Mary challenge the barrier to attending the required classes?
    She petitions for permission to attend, arguing for access to the education needed for her goal.
  9. Why does John Glenn request Katherine’s verification in the film?
    He trusts her expertise to check the electronic calculations before his orbital mission.
  10. What does the film’s ending suggest about the women’s continuing careers?
    Their contributions extend beyond one mission; the closing information connects the story to longer achievements.
  11. Compare one strategy used by two of the women.
    Accept a supported comparison of technical work, advocacy, education, or shared preparation.
  12. What is one limitation of the film’s historical timeline?
    It places career milestones together that occurred in different years; use NASA biographies to check dates.
  13. Choose one career detail to investigate. Which source would help?
    A named woman’s NASA biography can check a specific date, role, or contribution; distinguish source evidence from dialogue.
Online Worksheet Answer Key

Expected answers for 20 questions. Accept supported alternatives for analytical prompts.

  1. How can a workplace rule block opportunity even when someone has the needed skill?
    Rules about education, promotion, access, or facilities can exclude qualified people; ability alone does not remove a barrier.
  2. Why might a team check a machine’s calculations with human expertise?
    People can verify assumptions, detect errors, and assess whether results are reliable for a high-stakes task.
  3. What kind of work do the women initially perform at Langley?
    They perform mathematical calculations as human computers supporting aeronautical and space research.
  4. What assignment brings Katherine into the Space Task Group?
    Her mathematical skills lead to an assignment calculating and checking flight-related work for the group.
  5. Name one barrier Katherine faces in her new workplace.
    Accept restricted information, exclusion from meetings, unequal facilities, or dismissal of her expertise, with a scene.
  6. What responsibility does Dorothy carry without corresponding recognition in the film?
    She leads and supports the computing group while seeking official supervisory recognition.
  7. Why does Mary need additional classes to pursue engineering?
    The position’s qualifications require courses whose access is restricted by segregation.
  8. What does Katherine’s explanation of her long absences reveal?
    Segregated bathroom access costs time and imposes an unequal burden; this scene is a dramatization.
  9. Why does Dorothy decide to learn FORTRAN?
    She anticipates electronic computing and prepares herself and her coworkers for new kinds of work.
  10. How does Dorothy’s approach connect personal learning with collective support?
    She shares programming knowledge and advocates for the group rather than advancing only herself.
  11. How does Mary challenge the barrier to attending the required classes?
    She petitions for permission to attend, arguing for access to the education needed for her goal.
  12. Why does Katherine seek access to briefings and current information?
    Accurate changing calculations require timely information and participation rather than being kept outside the process.
  13. Why does John Glenn request Katherine’s verification in the film?
    He trusts her expertise to check the electronic calculations before his orbital mission.
  14. What does the launch sequence show about teamwork beyond the astronaut?
    Calculations, engineering, communication, and coordinated checks make the mission possible.
  15. How does the film show professional recognition changing?
    Katherine’s work becomes more visible, Dorothy moves into programming leadership, and Mary advances toward engineering.
  16. What does the film’s ending suggest about the women’s continuing careers?
    Their contributions extend beyond one mission; the closing information connects the story to longer achievements.
  17. Compare one strategy used by two of the women.
    Accept a supported comparison of technical work, advocacy, education, or shared preparation.
  18. Why would it be incomplete to explain their success only as hard work?
    Skill and persistence matter, but access, institutional change, collaboration, and recognition also shape opportunity.
  19. What is one limitation of the film’s historical timeline?
    It places career milestones together that occurred in different years; use NASA biographies to check dates.
  20. Choose one career detail to investigate. Which source would help?
    A named woman’s NASA biography can check a specific date, role, or contribution; distinguish source evidence from dialogue.

Final writing: Look for a clear claim, two accurately described scenes, reasoning, and a meaningful limitation of the portrayal or a question for further study. Accept different supported interpretations. No graphic descriptions are required.
